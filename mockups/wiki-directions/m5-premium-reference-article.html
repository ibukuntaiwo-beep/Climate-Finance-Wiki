The debt instrument funding the climate transition

A green bond is a fixed-income instrument — essentially an IOU — where the money raised is earmarked exclusively for projects with environmental benefits. The issuer (a government, bank, or corporation) borrows money from investors and promises to pay it back with interest, just like any conventional bond. The difference: the proceeds must fund eligible green projects.

How the Market Grew

The green bond market has grown from $11 billion in 2013 to over $580 billion in 2023. That growth reflects both genuine climate commitment and market opportunity — green bonds often price tighter than conventional equivalents (a phenomenon called the ‘greenium’), meaning issuers can borrow slightly cheaper by going green.

The European Investment Bank issued the first green bond in 2007, calling it a ‘Climate Awareness Bond.’ It raised €600 million. Since then, the market has expanded to include sovereigns (national governments), corporates, municipalities, and development banks. China, the EU, and the United States are the three largest markets by issuance volume.

Comms Insight

The existence of a greenium matters because it means the market is actively rewarding climate-aligned borrowing. When your organisation issues a green bond, the story isn’t just environmental — it’s financial. The market is paying a premium for credible green credentials.

The Framework

Green bonds follow one of several frameworks, most commonly the ICMA Green Bond Principles. These principles define four core components: use of proceeds, process for project evaluation and selection, management of proceeds, and reporting. Compliance is voluntary but has become a market expectation — bonds issued without adherence to recognised principles face higher scrutiny and may trade at a discount.

A Second-Party Opinion (SPO) from an independent reviewer — such as Sustainalytics, CICERO, or ISS ESG — has become the standard way issuers demonstrate credibility. The SPO assesses whether the bond’s framework aligns with the Green Bond Principles and whether the issuer’s environmental claims are credible.

What to Watch

The EU Green Bond Standard (EU GBS), finalised in 2023, represents the most significant regulatory intervention in the market to date. Unlike the voluntary ICMA principles, the EU GBS creates a formal standard with mandatory external review and post-issuance reporting. While adoption is optional, it signals a broader shift from voluntary self-labelling toward regulated green finance.

For comms professionals monitoring this space, three developments matter most: the convergence of voluntary and mandatory standards, the expansion of the ‘green’ label to adjacent categories (blue bonds for ocean conservation, transition bonds for high-emitting sectors), and growing regulatory attention to greenwashing in financial products.
