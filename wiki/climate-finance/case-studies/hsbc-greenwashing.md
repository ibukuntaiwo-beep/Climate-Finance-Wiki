# HSBC Greenwashing

> A UK advertising regulator banned HSBC ads for promoting tree-planting while the bank continued financing fossil fuels at scale — the first greenwashing ruling against a major bank.

## What Happened

In October 2021, HSBC ran a poster campaign across bus stops in London and Bristol. One ad featured an image of waves crashing on a shore with the text: *"Climate change doesn't do borders. Neither do rising sea levels. That's why HSBC is aiming to provide up to $1 trillion in financing and investment globally to help our clients transition to net zero."* A second poster showed tree rings with the message that HSBC was helping plant two million trees in the UK, locking in 1.25 million tonnes of carbon over their lifetime.

The UK Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) received complaints and investigated. In October 2022, it upheld those complaints and banned both ads. The ruling found that the ads were misleading because they made unqualified claims about HSBC's environmental work while omitting material information: that the bank was simultaneously financing companies and industries that emit significant greenhouse gases. Consumers would reasonably understand from the ads that HSBC was making a net positive environmental contribution — which the ASA found to be false.

HSBC was ordered not to run the ads again and was told that future environmental claims must be adequately qualified and must not omit information about the bank's role in financing carbon-intensive sectors. The ASA added an explicit warning to other banks that they should treat this ruling as a signal of how it would approach similar cases.

## Why It Matters for Comms

This case is one of the clearest illustrations of what [[Greenwashing Typology]] categorises as "greenlighting" — spotlighting a small, genuinely positive activity (tree-planting) to distract from far larger harmful ones (fossil fuel financing). HSBC's tree-planting programme was real. The $1 trillion net-zero commitment was real. The problem was presenting them without context, in a way that gave consumers a false overall impression.

For communications professionals, the ruling draws a precise line: you can communicate genuine positive action, but you cannot do so in a way that creates a misleading picture of the whole. Context is not optional. An ad that is factually accurate in its individual claims can still be found misleading if it omits information that materially changes how an audience would assess the company.

The ruling also established that advertising regulators — not just financial regulators — have standing to act on greenwashing claims. This widened the accountability landscape considerably. A company's comms team, not just its compliance team, now carries live regulatory risk when making environmental claims in public-facing materials.

## Key Facts

- October 2021: HSBC posters appear on bus stops in London and Bristol
- October 2022: ASA upholds complaints and bans both ads
- First greenwashing ruling by the ASA against a major bank
- Ads highlighted tree-planting and $1 trillion net-zero financing commitment
- Ruling: ads misleading for omitting HSBC's continued fossil fuel financing
- ASA explicitly warned other banks the ruling set a precedent
