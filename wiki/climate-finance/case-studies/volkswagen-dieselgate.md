# Volkswagen Dieselgate

> The defining corporate greenwashing case of the modern era — a company that built a fraud into its products while marketing them as clean.

## What Happened

In September 2015, the US Environmental Protection Agency issued a notice of violation to Volkswagen, revealing that the company had secretly programmed its diesel engines to cheat emissions tests. The software — called a "defeat device" — activated emissions controls only when a vehicle was being tested in a laboratory. On the road, those same cars emitted up to 40 times the legal limit of nitrogen oxides (NOx), a harmful air pollutant.

The deception affected approximately 11 million cars worldwide, including 500,000 in the United States, spanning model years 2009 through 2015. The vehicles were sold under Volkswagen's "Clean Diesel" marketing campaign, which claimed the cars reduced NOx emissions by 90% and were cleaner than petrol alternatives. The FTC later found they emitted up to 4,000% more NOx than the legal limit.

In January 2017, Volkswagen pleaded guilty to three criminal felony counts. Total settlements reached $14.7 billion in the US alone — covering consumer buybacks, environmental remediation, and civil penalties — making it one of the largest corporate fraud settlements in history. VW's then-CEO Martin Winterkorn resigned days after the story broke.

## Why It Matters for Comms

Dieselgate is the case that made "greenwashing" a mainstream term in boardrooms and newsrooms. Before 2015, greenwashing was mostly discussed in academic and NGO circles. Volkswagen's scandal — a company that didn't just exaggerate green credentials but engineered fraud into its products — showed that the reputational and legal consequences could be catastrophic and irreversible.

For communications professionals, the case carries a lasting lesson: the integrity of environmental claims must be verifiable. VW's marketing was technically sophisticated and thoroughly documented — which made the exposure worse, not better. The paper trail of "Clean Diesel" ads became evidence. Every claim a company makes about its environmental performance creates a future accountability surface.

The case also marked the beginning of aggressive regulator interest in corporate environmental claims. The FTC, EPA, and DOJ all had roles in the US action; European authorities pursued parallel cases. This set the precedent for the wave of [[Greenwashing]] enforcement that followed across financial services, advertising, and investment products.

## Key Facts

- September 2015: EPA issues notice of violation
- ~11 million cars worldwide fitted with defeat device software
- Real-world NOx emissions up to 40× the legal limit
- "Clean Diesel" campaign claimed 90% NOx reduction
- January 2017: VW pleads guilty to three criminal felony counts
- $14.7 billion total US settlement (consumer buybacks + environmental + civil penalties)
- Additional penalties in Germany and across Europe
- CEO Martin Winterkorn resigned; several executives charged criminally
