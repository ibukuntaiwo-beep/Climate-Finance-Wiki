# Marfrig Transition Bond

> A Brazilian meatpacker raised $500 million by labelling its bond as a "transition" instrument to clean up its beef supply chain — but investigations later found proceeds were used to buy cattle linked to illegal Amazon deforestation.

## What Happened

Marfrig is one of Brazil's largest beef producers and among the world's biggest meatpackers. In 2019, it issued a $500 million, 10-year transition bond at a yield of 6.625% — one of the first transition bonds from the agriculture sector. The bond was three times oversubscribed. In its publicly available framework, Marfrig stated that proceeds would fund traceability in its cattle supply chain to mitigate the risk of sourcing from deforested land.

The controversy emerged in stages. Investigations by Mongabay and other outlets found that a substantial portion of bond proceeds — approximately $1 billion in total cattle purchases — went to feedlots run by Marcos Antonio Molina dos Santos, Marfrig's own board chairman. More critically, cattle from those feedlots were later traced back to ranches in illegally deforested areas of the Amazon and Cerrado. Marfrig's traceability system, it turned out, had a structural blind spot: it tracked direct suppliers but not the "indirect" suppliers — breeding ranches — from which cattle entered the supply chain a step earlier.

FAIRR, an investor network focused on food system risks, published an assessment noting that while Marfrig's stated objectives were credible, the bond didn't go far enough beyond business-as-usual spending and lacked indicators showing whether improvements were actually achieved. As of 2025, Marfrig faces ongoing investor pressure and scrutiny.

## Why It Matters for Comms

Marfrig illustrates the specific credibility challenge of [[Transition Bonds]]: since there is no universally accepted taxonomy for what counts as a "transition" activity, the label is only as strong as the underlying framework, oversight, and verification. The bond's stated goal — supply chain traceability — was genuinely relevant to Marfrig's environmental impact. The problem was that the mechanism for achieving it was inadequate, and the use of proceeds created a conflict of interest.

For communications professionals, the case raises a question that clients increasingly face: when is a transition story credible, and when is it a reputational liability? The presence of a bond framework and third-party review is not sufficient on its own. What matters is whether the activities funded represent a genuine step change from current practice, whether conflicts of interest are disclosed and managed, and whether the progress metrics are independently verifiable.

The beef sector context matters too. Cattle ranching is one of the leading drivers of Amazon deforestation — which makes any "sustainability" claim from a meatpacker extraordinarily scrutinised. Communicators working with clients in agriculture, food, or any sector with high land-use impact need to hold their clients' transition claims to a higher standard of evidence, precisely because the reputational downside of failure is severe.

## Key Facts

- 2019: Marfrig issues $500M transition bond, 3× oversubscribed
- Stated purpose: fund traceability in cattle supply chain to prevent deforestation-linked sourcing
- ~$1 billion of proceeds used to buy cattle from feedlots run by Marfrig's own board chairman
- Later investigations traced cattle from those feedlots to illegally deforested areas
- Structural flaw: traceability system covered direct but not indirect suppliers
- FAIRR: bond objectives credible but targets inadequate to show real improvement
- No universally accepted taxonomy for "transition" bonds at time of issuance
