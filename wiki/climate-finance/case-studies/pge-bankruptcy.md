# PG&E Bankruptcy

> California's largest utility filed for bankruptcy in 2019 after wildfires caused by its equipment triggered $30 billion in liabilities — the starkest demonstration that physical climate risk can destroy a company's capital structure.

## What Happened

Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) is California's largest electricity and gas utility, serving roughly 16 million people in northern and central California. In November 2018, the Camp Fire — ignited by a faulty PG&E transmission line — became the deadliest and most destructive wildfire in California history. It killed 85 people and destroyed the town of Paradise. PG&E's equipment was also linked to the Tubbs Fire (2017) and multiple other major blazes.

In January 2019, PG&E filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. It faced 750 civil lawsuits and estimated wildfire liabilities of up to $30 billion — far more than its insurance coverage. The company ultimately reached a $13.5 billion settlement with wildfire victims. It emerged from bankruptcy in 2020 but has remained under intense scrutiny, subject to state oversight and a probation agreement.

The legal mechanism that made PG&E uniquely vulnerable was California's doctrine of **inverse condemnation**: under state law, utilities are strictly liable for damage caused by their equipment, even without proof of negligence. If a power line starts a fire, the utility pays — regardless of whether it maintained the line reasonably. As climate change extends California's wildfire season and intensifies fire conditions, this doctrine turned a physical climate risk into an existential financial one.

## Why It Matters for Comms

Columbia University's Climate School described PG&E's bankruptcy as "the first climate change bankruptcy" — the moment when the theoretical link between rising temperatures and financial collapse became concrete. Climate change was identified as a material factor in the wildfires: longer droughts, stronger winds, and higher maximum temperatures created the tinderbox conditions PG&E's infrastructure couldn't survive.

For communications professionals, the case is the most powerful available proof point that [[Physical Risks]] are financial risks rather than abstract future concerns. When a client or journalist asks why investors care about climate, PG&E is the answer: a utility worth tens of billions of dollars was wiped out not by policy change or market shift, but by the weather.

The case also has a disclosure dimension. Investors and analysts were caught largely off-guard — traditional utility valuation models did not price in catastrophic wildfire liability. It accelerated regulatory interest in mandatory climate risk disclosure, particularly for asset-intensive sectors with physical exposure. The TCFD and IFRS S2 frameworks' emphasis on physical risk scenarios owes something to cases like this one.

## Key Facts

- November 2018: Camp Fire (linked to PG&E equipment) — 85 deaths, town of Paradise destroyed
- January 2019: PG&E files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy
- Estimated liabilities: up to $30 billion across wildfire claims
- $13.5 billion settlement with wildfire victims
- 750 civil lawsuits at time of filing
- Legal mechanism: California's inverse condemnation doctrine (strict liability regardless of negligence)
- Columbia Climate School called it "the first climate change bankruptcy"
- PG&E emerged from bankruptcy in 2020 under state supervision
