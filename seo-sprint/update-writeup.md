# Climate Finance Wiki: May 2026 Update

The Climate Finance Wiki has had two significant updates since launch. The case studies section has expanded from 8 to 12 stories, covering the full range of how climate finance plays out in boardrooms, courtrooms, and capital markets. And every article on the site, all 52 of them, now includes verified source links to the official documents, reports, and institutions behind the claims.

Here is what changed, why, and what it means for readers.

---

## Four new case studies

The wiki launched with eight case studies. That covered the basics, but left gaps. The four new additions fill them:

**Engine No. 1 vs ExxonMobil.** A hedge fund with a $40 million stake and 0.02% ownership unseated three board members at the world's largest oil company in May 2021. The case shows that shareholder activism on climate is no longer a symbolic exercise. Ownership, even a tiny stake, can be leverage.

**BlackRock's Climate Ultimatum.** In January 2020, CEO Larry Fink used his annual letter to tell every public company CEO that climate risk is investment risk. When the world's largest asset manager ($7 trillion at the time) puts climate on the agenda, it changes the calculus for every company that wants to remain investable.

**Norway Divests from Oil.** Norway's $1.3 trillion Government Pension Fund Global, the world's largest sovereign wealth fund, excluded upstream oil and gas companies in 2019. A petro-state divesting from its own industry is the strongest possible signal that the financial logic of fossil fuel investment has shifted.

**The Great Coal Exit.** Between 2013 and 2023, more than 200 financial institutions stopped financing coal, starting with the World Bank and spreading to major banks and insurers across Europe. It is the most complete proof that stranded asset theory has moved from forecast to fact.

Together, the 12 case studies now cover four distinct types of climate finance action: shareholder activism, institutional signalling, sovereign divestment, and systemic market shift. Readers can see how climate risk translates into real consequences across different contexts and scales.

No Wikipedia. No paywalled articles. No PDFs likely to move.

## How every link was checked

AI-generated URLs are unreliable. A plausible-looking link to a UN report might point to a page that has never existed. To prevent this, every source went through two levels of verification:

1. **Automated check.** Each URL was tested with an HTTP request using browser-like headers. Links had to return a valid response and land on the correct page, not a generic homepage or error screen.

2. **Manual review.** Each batch of sources was reviewed before being written into the articles. Paywalled sources were replaced with free alternatives. Wikipedia links were upgraded to primary institutional sources. Dead links were caught and swapped.

Out of the initial 189 proposed URLs, several were caught and replaced during verification: an AXA page that had moved, Bloomberg and Reuters articles behind paywalls, a UN appointment page with an incorrect URL, and a handful of sites that block automated requests but are confirmed live in a browser.
