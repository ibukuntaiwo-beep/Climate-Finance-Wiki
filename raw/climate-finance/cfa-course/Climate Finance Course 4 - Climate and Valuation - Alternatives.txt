Climate Finance Course 4 - Climate and Valuation: Alternatives

MODULE 1: Private Equity

Lesson 1: Strengths and Weaknesses of Managing Private Equity in Relation to Climate Change

Private equity (PE) differs from public equity by offering investors more control and access to company information, enabling tailored climate strategies and operational influence.

Equity investment, whether in public or private companies, has historically exhibited higher returns at the expense of greater risk compared with debt. The potential payoff for equity
investors is unlimited upside, with downside limited to the amount invested.

A key difference between private and public equity is that, because of the significant size of shareholdings, private equity ownership allows more direct control over decisions than public equity.

Private equity does not usually invest in startups or fast-growing firms. That’s more for venture capital.

PE investment strategies include:
Venture capital (early-stage climate tech, renewables, agri-tech, green transport),
Growth capital (expansion-focused funding), and
Leveraged buyouts (LBOs) (acquiring mature companies using debt).

PE investors aim for returns via dividends and exits, with potential upside from illiquidity premiums, leverage, and active management involvement.

Most of the key challenges in climate finance originate from the significant financing requirements, as well as the technological advancement required to meet net zero targets.
On top of that, technical assistance availability and the associated human talent capacity are also common bottlenecks. Within private equity investment, additional challenges are a lower level of data transparency and data availability.

Climate finance opportunities include alignment with net-zero targets, access to growing sustainable investment capital, and appeal to ESG-focused limited partners (LPs).
Key strengths in climate-aligned PE investing include:
Long-term holding periods,
Strong due diligence,
Control in portfolio companies,
Tailored investment strategies,
Board-level engagement, and
Attracting climate-conscious talent.
Weaknesses and risks include:
Data opacity and inconsistency,
Shorter holding periods vs. sovereign/pension funds,
Difficult climate-related asset valuations,
Greenwashing, and
Limited standardization in LP-GP climate reporting agreements.
Driving forces behind PE climate adaptation include:
Increasing LP capital allocation to green firms,
Support from development finance institutions (DFIs),
Net-zero regulatory frameworks, and
Multi-stakeholder collaboration (e.g., sovereign wealth co-investment).
Barriers include growing emissions from expanding portfolio companies, greenwashing skepticism, and fewer disclosure obligations compared to public equity.
Innovative methods adopted by PE practitioners include:
Tailored climate investment policies,
Enhanced climate due diligence (aligned with IFRS S2),
Capacity building via PRI technical guides, and
Adoption of governance protocols to combat greenwashing risks.
A six-step greenwashing governance framework (KPMG) includes risk awareness, appetite setting, governance structures, training, tech adoption (e.g., AI), and crisis preparation.


Lesson 2 - Key Tools and Metrics for Valuation of Private Equity
The UNPRI technical guide “TCFD for Private Equity General Partners” provides a detailed framework to help GPs implement climate-related risk management aligned with TCFD's four pillars: Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics & Targets.
Six priority objectives include raising climate awareness, establishing governance systems, conducting materiality assessments, integrating climate into investment processes, and conducting annual reviews.
Practical implementation is structured into three phases, including pre-acquisition due diligence, post-acquisition engagement, and exit planning, each supported by tools like climate valuation models and climate KPIs.
GPs should define specific climate performance indicators for each portfolio company and tailor climate resilience strategies based on materiality and exposure levels.
A Net Zero Investment Framework for private equity includes four target types:
Portfolio coverage targets (e.g., financed emissions),
Engagement thresholds (e.g., 100% action on relevant holdings),
Allocation to climate solutions (e.g., percent of capital),
Decarbonization targets covering Scope 1–3 emissions.
A hypothetical case study of a steel company acquisition illustrates how GPs adjust valuations for transition risks (e.g., EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism) by factoring in future capex and regulatory compliance costs, reducing firm valuation.
Post-acquisition climate strategies include:
Creating tailored ESG/climate policies,
Assigning roles and resources,
Establishing board-level accountability, and
Organizing cross-portfolio climate learning sessions.
Ongoing monitoring practices involve site visits, performance tracking, climate KPI reporting, and adherence to ESG covenants, supported by diagrams like Engagement Interaction Flows and Monitoring Matrices.
GPs use core and tailored KPIs (e.g., carbon intensity, job creation, water use) to monitor environmental and social impacts, rolled up to the fund level.
The LP–GP relationship plays a crucial role in climate accountability through:
Due diligence questionnaires (DDQs),
ESG clauses in fund terms, and
Regular sustainability reporting.
Case studies from Swedfund and Sawari Ventures highlight best practices in ESG integration, standardized reporting, and climate engagement across challenging markets.
Climate considerations throughout the investment lifecycle—especially valuation impact modeling—are critical to identifying risks, enhancing transparency, and improving portfolio resilience.

Lesson 3 - Integrating Climate Risks and Opportunities in the Private Equity Investment Process
Tools and metrics are utilized to assess private equity investments, with a specific focus on incorporating climate risks and opportunities.
Private equity’s long-term investment strategies necessitate considering climate-related factors to achieve sustainable value creation.
The TCFD framework is commonly utilized by private equity managers for incorporating climate-related risks and opportunities into investment decisions.
Case studies, such as the one of Old Mutual Alternative Investments (OMAI), illustrate the practical implementation of integrating environmental, social, and governance (ESG) principles, including climate change considerations, into investment decision making.
Scenario analysis, a key component of the TCFD framework, enables better understanding of the specific climate risks faced by companies according to different future climate change trajectories.
The PCRAM is highlighted as a tool for assessing and integrating physical climate risks into infrastructure investment decision making, contributing to long-term sustainability and risk mitigation.
The Coalition for Climate Resilient Investment (CCRI) promotes collaboration between private finance and public institutions to better assess and manage physical climate risks, facilitating investment in climate-resilient infrastructure.


MODULE 2: Private Debt

Lesson 1 - Key Attributes of Private Debt Ecosystem

Private debt refers to loans provided directly to private companies or projects, distinct from public markets and typically unrated and illiquid.
The market has grown significantly post-2008 as private lending funds filled the gap left by banks due to regulatory tightening.
Private debt strategies include:
Direct lending (senior secured loans),
Mezzanine (subordinated debt with equity options),
Venture debt, and
Distressed debt (high-return, capital-appreciation focused).
Investments offer higher potential returns than traditional fixed income due to illiquidity premiums, custom covenants, and alpha-driven management.
Key features include:
Closed-end fund structures,
Bespoke lending terms,
Floating rate returns,
Mark-to-model valuation,
Tailored covenants, and
Sub-investment grade risk levels.
Private debt exhibits low correlation with public equities and bonds, providing diversification benefits; e.g., correlation with Australian equity index is –0.08.
Returns stem from cash income (interest) and capital appreciation, depending on the strategy—senior lending emphasizes income; distressed debt targets capital gains.
Investor motivations include:
Higher order of claim in capital structure,
Certainty of income streams,
ESG-linked incentives (e.g., margin reductions for GHG reductions),
Access to impact-driven debt with influence on sustainability behavior.
Disadvantages of private debt include:
Large minimum ticket sizes (USD 10–50M),
Limited issuer influence,
Illiquidity (no secondary market),
Prepayment risk if issuers refinance at lower rates.
Issuers are drawn to private debt for:
Non-dilutive financing (no equity loss),
Structural independence (no governance interference), and
Predictability and confidentiality in capital raising.
Underwriters (e.g., banks) are motivated by:
Fee income,
League table rankings, and
ESG branding through sustainable debt structuring.
The private debt market now includes non-traditional borrowers, such as:
Emerging markets,
Sustainability-focused firms, and
Climate adaptation projects (e.g., desalination, food, housing).
Climate elements are increasingly embedded in debt covenants, aligning private debt with investor sustainability mandates and broadening the ESG debt universe.


Lesson 2 - Characteristics of Private Debt Instruments in Relation to Climate Change

Debt financing for climate-related projects spans three phases of renewable energy (RE) development: development, construction, and operations/maintenance, with instruments aligned to each phase's risk profile.
Major debt categories include:
Corporate debt facilities: e.g., equity bridge loans, construction loans
Main debt facilities: e.g., export credit agency (ECA) credit, project finance loans
Ancillary facilities: e.g., working capital loans, letters of credit
Project bonds: used in post-construction refinancing
Climate-Aligned Features of Debt Instruments:
Green loans finance defined green projects under LMA/APLMA standards, requiring strict tracking and reporting of use-of-proceeds.
Sustainability-linked loans (SLLs) tie financial terms to achieving KPIs (e.g., GHG reduction, energy efficiency). Performance impacts pricing, rewarding sustainability improvements.
Mini-perm loans (5–7 years) help manage rising rates and regulatory pressures (e.g., Basel III Endgame) by front-loading financing with balloon payments to reduce short-term cost of funds.
Portfolio financing allows bundling of smaller RE projects (e.g., solar, wind, BESS) to diversify risks, gain scale, and access better financing terms. Common in C&I solar.
Stapled financing, used in clean energy M&A, offers pre-arranged debt packages to bidders—popular in ASEAN and the Middle East to streamline investment processes.
Instrument-Specific Attributes:
Construction facilities: Short-term (1–4 yrs), bullet repayment, often sponsor-recourse.
Equity bridge loans: Boost sponsor IRR by deferring equity injection; limited to equity portion.
ECA credit: Long-term, export-tied, low-interest loans that may include political risk insurance.
Project finance loans: Long-term, non-recourse, cash-flow-backed—ideal for large RE infrastructure.
Project bonds: Institutional-grade refinancing post-construction; requires derisked, rated assets.
Working capital & LC/guarantees: Ancillary tools for liquidity, contract support; often secured via sponsor relationships.
Emerging Trends:
Blended financing leverages both public and private capital to de-risk projects in emerging markets (via MDBs/DFIs such as EIB, ADB, IFC, etc.).
Greenwashing risks have increased scrutiny on alignment with Green Loan Principles and SLL Principles, making compliance and transparency critical.
Case Studies:
Case A: A Southeast Asian food/agriculture firm used a syndicated SLL facility, with margin reductions tied to achieving sustainability KPIs (e.g., energy and water efficiency).
Case B: An Australian RE developer packaged 1 GW of brownfield solar/wind into a mini-perm loan. Results: 14% equity IRR with 80/20 D/E, dropping to 10% with 50/50—showing trade-offs between risk, leverage, and return.


Lesson 3 - Use of Key Climate Tools and Metrics in Debt Origination

Financial institutions are integrating ESG considerations into debt origination to align with climate regulations and frameworks, influencing the risk-return profile of debt instruments.
Debt instrument assessment has evolved to include climate-related factors using various ESG regulations and frameworks such as the UN Sustainable Development Goals, the Paris Climate Agreement, and the EU Green Deal.
Financial institutions use third-party ESG rating tools from providers like Sustainalytics and MSCI to supplement internal assessments, helping to quantify unmanaged ESG risks and align with global standards.
Institutions adopt sector-specific net-zero pathways, guided by frameworks like that of Glasgow Financial Alliance for Net Zero (GFANZ), to ensure financed projects progressively align with net-zero emissions targets by 2050.
Issuers enhance debt offerings by including specific ESG-related reporting requirements, ensuring transparent communication on the impact of debt investments on climate change and sustainability.
The Sustainalytics ESG Risk Rating and Equator Principles example illustrates how institutions assess and manage the ESG impact of both corporate and project financing.
Climate and ESG factors now play a central role in the decision-making process for debt instruments, often determining whether a transaction proceeds to the assessment stage based on its alignment with prescribed climate and ESG guidelines.


Lesson 4 - Common Challenges in the ESG Private Debt Domain
ESG private debt markets are growing, but GSSSB products still account for less than 20% of annual debt issuance globally.
Key challenges in the ESG private debt domain include:
Evolving standards and benchmarks, especially in emerging markets.
Low correlation across ESG rating providers, creating inconsistency in assessments.
Greenwashing risks, where labeled green projects fail to deliver promised impact.
Weak governance, due to lack of unified global regulation.
Data limitations, making it hard to verify environmental impact and track performance.
Case Study A: Investor Framework (Ninety-One Emerging Africa Infrastructure Fund)
Uses SDG-linked impact criteria and a matrix to assess climate-aligned private debt investments.
Applies both qualitative and quantitative ESG filters, backed by a comprehensive ESG policy, risk, and reporting framework.
Targets returns slightly below conventional benchmarks (e.g., SOFR + 5%) but with high ESG impact.
Case Study B: Renewable Energy Portfolio Financing
A 1GW solar/wind portfolio in Australia added climate/ESG certification (Green Loan & Equator Principles) at a cost of AUD 5M.
Revenue from Large-Scale Generation Certificates (LGCs) added AUD 20M/year, boosting equity IRR from 14.3% to 19.0% and average DSCR from 1.43× to 1.56×.
Demonstrates that ESG-linked enhancements can improve both investor returns and lender confidence.


MODULE 3: Infrastructure
Lesson  - Climate Risk Assessment for Infrastructure Investments

Climate change poses significant risks to infrastructure assets, with varying degrees of impact on transportation and water systems.
Infrastructure investments face physical risks and transition risks.
Effective climate risk management for infrastructure involves assessing hazard exposure and vulnerability and developing resilience strategies to mitigate identified risks.
Methods like the European Investment Bank’s (EIB) three-stage climate risk assessment help evaluate the climate risks associated with specific infrastructure projects.
Institutions like the EIB assess climate risks across their entire portfolio, categorizing risks into dimensions such as credit risk, market risk, and operational risk over different time horizons.
Examples from the European Investment Bank (EIB) and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) illustrate methodologies for assessing and managing climate risks in infrastructure investments.


Lesson 2 - Infrastructure Finance: Resilience Planning for Infrastructure Investment
Resilience in infrastructure is the ability to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and quickly recover from climate-related impacts, ensuring the continuous functionality of critical systems.
Allocating resources for resilience analysis during the initial stages of project design and planning can result in substantial long-term savings and attract private investment by demonstrating low risk and high profitability.

One definition of resilience in the context of infrastructure is the ability to adapt and recover from shocks and stresses while maintaining or enhancing its essential functions.
When infrastructure is made resilient, it may be able to anticipate, absorb, adapt to, and quickly recover from a climate-related impact.
There is a widening gap in the funding required for robust and resilient new infrastructure in developing countries. A concern is that both repairing existing structures and building new ones contribute to greenhouse gases (GHGs) due to the heavy reliance on industries like cement and steel, which are major emitters.
To tackle these challenges, it is key to develop climate-resilient infrastructure that can endure the impacts of climate change without exacerbating emissions. This requires an approach that factors in climate-related risks to infrastructure, ensuring adaptation strategies do not negatively impact natural resources and biodiversity.

Resilience planning involves comparing the costs and benefits of resilience measures against the cost of doing nothing, incorporating climate cases into financial models to determine the economic viability of investments.
The Physical Climate Risk Assessment Methodology (PCRAM) is used to integrate climate risk assessments into infrastructure investment decisions. It focuses on direct impacts and is iteratively updated throughout the asset lifecycle.
The PCRAM process includes steps such as defining project scope, assessing materiality of risks, developing resilience strategies, and conducting economic analysis to inform investment decisions.
The wind power plant case study illustrates the application of the PCRAM, highlighting the assessment of risks like sea level rise and wind speed variations, and the evaluation of resilience measures.

Lesson 3 - ESG Performance Standards and Blended Finance

ESG standards are essential in infrastructure investments for assessing carbon footprint, biodiversity impact, waste management, community outcomes, and governance transparency.
Environmental standards emphasize GHG reduction, biodiversity protection, and resilience, often using nature-based solutions and circular economy principles.
Social standards cover labor rights, land use, stakeholder engagement, and gender equity, guided by frameworks such as the ILO Core Labour Standards and UN Human Rights Principles.
Governance standards focus on legal compliance, anti-corruption, and ethical oversight, strengthening long-term project accountability.
A case study using the Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) framework highlights a 100MW solar project’s review of fauna, flooding risks, land leasing impacts, and stakeholder engagement plans.
Leading ESG frameworks include:
IFC Performance Standards,
World Bank EHS Guidelines,
ISO environmental standards, and
Host-country regulations and treaties.
Blended finance mobilizes private capital for sustainable development by combining it with public or philanthropic funds—especially critical in developing markets.
Blended finance instruments include:
Equity (e.g., Tropical Asia Forest Fund 2),
Debt (e.g., La Jacinta solar project in Uruguay),
Guarantees/insurance (e.g., Seychelles Blue Bond),
Results-based financing (e.g., gender-linked incentives in Uruguay),
Grants and technical assistance (e.g., Africa GreenCo).
Projects often combine instruments in layered fund structures to manage risk over time—from development to construction to operations (e.g., FCCF, Climate Investor One)


Lesson 4 - Role of Green Infrastructure and Sustainable Development in Mitigating Climate Risks

Infrastructure significantly contributes to climate change, with transportation and energy generation infrastructure reliant on fossil fuels being major contributors.
Integrating sustainability into infrastructure projects benefits the infrastructure asset resilience and supports sustainable development.
Germany’s “Energiewende,” transitioning away from nuclear power to renewables, illustrates the long-term impact of infrastructure decisions.
Infrastructure projects have long-term implications that are challenging to alter once in place, as seen in Germany’s investment in liquified natural gas (LNG) infrastructure.
Addressing issues in infrastructure development requires attention at both project and national levels, including policies, regulations, and stakeholder capacity.
Programs like energy conservation initiatives in Thailand demonstrate successful national-level policies for increasing infrastructure efficiency.
Carbon pricing and shadow carbon pricing are tools used to value climate externalities and integrate them into infrastructure project valuations and decision-making processes.


MODULE 4: Real Estate

Lesson 1 - The Effects of Climate Change on Real Estate Investments

Real estate contributes approximately 35%–40% of global greenhouse gas emissions, primarily through fossil fuel use for heating and electricity consumption.
Climate change poses both physical risks, such as extreme weather events, and transition risks, including regulatory changes and market preferences.
Physical risks can lead to damage to buildings from events like coastal flooding, inland flooding, storms, and wildfires.
Transition risks stem from efforts to address climate change and the move toward a low-carbon economy, affecting asset values and market demand.
Real estate industry standards and frameworks, like the building research establishment environmental assessment method (BREEAM) and the global real estate sustainability benchmark (GRESB), help assess and improve sustainability practices.
Class A, B, and C office buildings differ in quality and vulnerability to climate risks, with older, less efficient buildings facing greater exposure.
Investors can respond to climate risks through adaptation measures, green retrofits, diversification, and potential divestment strategies.


Lesson 2 - Key Climate-Related Tools and Metrics Impacting Valuation of Real Estate
Climate risk tools in real estate assess both physical risks (e.g. floods, wildfires) and transition risks (e.g. policy changes, carbon regulation) across the investment lifecycle.
Physical risk metrics include flood and heat maps, climate due diligence scans, location-based risk scores, and modeling of extreme weather events and heat stress.
Transition risk metrics include:
GRESB assessments,
Green certifications (e.g. LEED, BREEAM),
CRREM pathways,
Carbon emissions tracking,
Net-zero goal monitoring, and
Regulatory compliance tools (e.g. SFDR, TCFD).
Real estate energy performance is benchmarked through EPCs, ENERGY STAR ratings, and national mandates like the EU Energy Performance of Buildings Directive.
Sector-specific tools and risks vary: offices and retail sectors lead in adaptation via green leases and building retrofits, while sectors like data centers face rising scrutiny over energy use, water consumption, e-waste, and Scope 2 emissions.
Case Study A – ABC Real Estate:
Used risk mapping and carbon footprinting to identify high-risk properties.
Retrofitting analysis showed a USD 18.8M NPV in the base case, still viable at USD 1.7M under conservative assumptions.
Strategic retrofits target 30% emissions and energy reductions.
Case Study B – Data Centers:
Face high energy and water demands, growing carbon emissions, e-waste challenges, and fragmented regulations.
Emerging best practices include closed-loop cooling, renewable energy, and LEED certifications.
Investors are advised to:
Use scenario analysis, GIS tools, and location-specific models;
Integrate ESG into due diligence and financial modeling;
Diversify portfolios geographically and by climate exposure.
Best practices for portfolio-level risk management include:
Quantify climate risk using financial tools,
Seek expert input and partner with data providers,
Engage stakeholders continuously across the investment lifecycle.

Lesson 3: Sustainable Building Practices and Green Certification
Over 600 green building certification programs exist globally, tailored to specific markets like Japan's comprehensive assessment system for built environment efficiency (CASBEE) and Australia’s Green Star.
Notable certifications include BREEAM (launched in 1990) and LEED (launched in 1998), which focus on environmental factors such as energy and water use.
Edge (launched in 2014) offers “Zero Carbon” certification and a free app for assessing building performance.
Green certification can incur costs of 10%–30% over standard builds but offers benefits like reduced operating costs and increased property values.
BREEAM and LEED differ in assessment methods, certification thresholds, and overall approach to sustainability.
The BREEAM certification process involves licensed assessors, while LEED relies on data supplied by the developer.
Real estate investors are increasingly integrating climate risk mitigation into their portfolios through investment in low-carbon infrastructure and building retrofits, guided by interim goals and various tools and frameworks for measurement and reporting.
