Climate Finance Course 5 - Portfolio Management and Stewardship for Climate Risk

MODULE 1: Integrating Climate Consideration in Portfolio Management


Lesson 1: Approaches for Integrating Climate Risks and Opportunities

Investors can integrate climate risks and opportunities using macro, factor, sector-level, and bottom-up fundamental approaches.

Climate solutions can be characterized in three different manners.3
• Low carbon. Activities with close to zero emissions that already make a substantial contribution to achieving net zero (e.g., the leasing of passenger vehicles with zero tailpipe CO 2 emissions).
• Transitional. Activities that make a substantial contribution to the transition to net zero by reducing their own emissions, even if they are not yet low carbon (e.g., the manufacture of cement with CO 2 emissions intensity below a specific threshold), and the leasing of vessels with a large percentage of energy from zero-carbon fuels.
• Enabling. Activities that are enabling emissions reductions in the wider economy (e.g., the manufacture of energy-efficient equipment for buildings) and infrastructure for low-carbon road transport such as electric vehicle (EV) charging points.

Macro-driven models assess systemic climate impacts on GDP, inflation, and asset prices across the economy using scenario tools like NGFS and IPR.
Factor models aim to isolate “climate beta” or climate risk premia, but challenges include short climate data history and overlap with traditional risk factors.
Climate beta measures portfolio sensitivity to climate risks; some green ETFs have high carbon footprints but low (even negative) climate betas due to resilience to transition risks.
Sector-level/thematic strategies focus on areas like clean energy or circular economy, but may miss macro spillover effects from other sectors.
Fundamental (bottom-up) approaches assess issuer-specific risks (e.g., physical or transition risk) and allow deeper analysis of asset- or company-level exposures.
Strategic asset allocation models vary in how well they capture climate risks:
Black-Litterman is favored for accommodating pricing uncertainty.
Factor risk allocation and total portfolio approaches allow integration of climate-specific risk factors.
Liability-driven investing (LDI) and mean-variance optimization (MVO) are less climate-sensitive due to their dependence on baseline assumptions.
Regime-switching models may be useful for capturing abrupt climate-related shifts but are not yet widely used.
Climate risk is both systemic and localized—affecting global financial systems and specific assets—requiring a multifaceted integration strategy in portfolio construction.


Lesson 2: Frameworks for Portfolio Decarbonization

The Net Zero Investment Framework (NZIF 2.0) guides investors aligning portfolios with 1.5°C climate goals, emphasizing governance, strategic asset allocation, engagement, and policy advocacy.
NZIF 2.0 introduces four core decarbonization targets:
Asset alignment: Long-term real-world decarbonization via asset selection and engagement.
Engagement threshold: Requires that 70% of financed emissions are either aligned or being actively engaged.
Portfolio decarbonization reference: Optional intensity or absolute reduction target over 10 years.
Allocation to climate solutions: Measurable investment in climate-positive assets.
GFANZ and IIGCC recommend various portfolio alignment metrics:
Binary Target Metric (BTM) – % of companies with validated targets.
Maturity Scale Alignment (MSA) – Qualitative/quantitative scale from “aligned” to “not aligned.”
Benchmark Divergence (BMD) – Compares company emissions to a 1.5°C pathway.
Implied Temperature Rise (ITR) – Translates alignment into a temperature outcome, but is controversial due to data/model limitations.
Academic findings show ESG and clean energy funds tend to avoid high-emission sectors, possibly hampering the financing of their transition efforts.
Carbon-constrained portfolio optimization can overweight or underweight assets based on emission intensity while preserving risk-return characteristics.
Only ~7% of companies in MSCI ACWI are aligned with 1.5°C; most are >2°C, highlighting the difficulty of building climate-aligned portfolios without tracking error.
Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs), like the DICE model, are increasingly explored to guide long-term climate-aligned investment strategies, though they face criticisms for oversimplification and idealized assumptions.
IAMs incorporate Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) and Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) to simulate climate futures and investment scenarios.
Institutions like the Columbia Center on Sustainable Investment are designing investment frameworks using IAMs to allocate capital proportionally across sectors and regions for net-zero alignment.

Lesson 3: Role of Climate-Related Data and Metrics in Portfolio Decision Making
Climate constraints can be integrated into equity portfolio optimization with minimal impact on risk or performance.
Carbon intensity data is highly skewed, with 40% of emissions concentrated in 100 firms, enabling strategic targeting of high emitters.
Optimization can follow a “trim many” or “sell few” approach to reduce emissions while maintaining risk balance.
Tightening carbon constraints reduces signal exposure and portfolio overlap but remains manageable up to ~40% below benchmark.
Sector composition can stay within ±5% of benchmark weight under moderate carbon budgets, avoiding significant sector tilt.
Quantitative models show up to 20% carbon reduction achieves 97% overlap and 99.8% return correlation with unconstrained portfolios.
Energy and materials sectors have high carbon intensity but low portfolio weights, making them key leverage points in decarbonization.
Alpha exposure (idiosyncratic signal strength) remains stable even with carbon constraints, supporting efficient climate-aligned optimization.
Green firms tend to outperform “brown” peers over time—a phenomenon known as the equity greenium.
Style factors like value, quality, and volatility remain stable across carbon-reduced simulations, indicating no meaningful style drift.

Lesson 5: Risk Return Dynamics of Portfolio Optimization in Fixed Income

Green bond issuance reached USD 575 billion in 2023, driven by strong demand and issuer preference for debt over equity.
The “greenium” reflects the premium investors pay for green bonds due to limited supply and use-of-proceeds conditions that reduce greenwashing risk.
Climate risk exposure varies by maturity—longer-term bonds are more vulnerable to interest rate and climate risks, while short-term bonds are less exposed but still affected by sector-specific shocks.
Investment-grade (IG) portfolios are more exposed to climate shocks than high-yield (HY) portfolios, due to higher fossil fuel exposure and longer duration.
Utilities and energy sectors show the highest climate-implied spread changes, even for bonds with short maturities.
Divergent net-zero scenarios show the most severe valuation losses across fixed income sectors, with IG portfolios seeing greater climate spread shifts than HY.
Climate stress tests reveal regional differences—climate impacts are more significant in U.S. credit markets than in Europe.
IG’s higher exposure to fossil sub-sectors and longer durations leads to greater vulnerability despite better credit quality.
Portfolio alignment with Paris goals can improve valuations in climate-sensitive sectors, but sector and maturity mix remains a key determinant of risk.
Credit investors must consider tenor, spread sensitivity, and sectoral allocation when integrating climate risks into fixed income strategies.


Lesson 6: Climate Benchmarks and Approaches to Managing Passive Portfolios
The EU introduced two official climate benchmarks: the Climate Transition Benchmark (CTB) and the Paris-Aligned Benchmark (PAB) to promote decarbonization and reduce greenwashing.
PABs exclude fossil fuel-heavy companies, while CTBs take a sector-neutral approach, allowing for transition participation across all industries.
Both benchmarks align with the IPCC’s 1.5°C scenario and target a minimum 7% annual decarbonization rate, covering Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions.
Minimum standards include exclusions (e.g., coal, oil, and controversial weapons), green/brown revenue thresholds, and disqualification for non-alignment with decarbonization pathways.
The HSBC MSCI Climate Paris-Aligned ETF demonstrates how passive products apply PAB standards using climate VaR, green–brown ratios, and low carbon transition scores.
Passive investing is growing rapidly, with passive ESG fund flows surpassing active flows as of 2023, especially in PAB- and CTB-linked funds.
Passive strategies offer low cost and diversification benefits but face challenges such as reliance on backward-looking data, transition risk exposure, and limited adaptability during volatility.
Climate-aligned passive portfolios may experience tracking error volatility, especially during global disruptions (e.g., energy crises), with PABs more exposed due to their strict exclusions.
Academic studies project persistently higher tracking errors in climate-aligned portfolios through 2050, particularly in emerging markets with fewer resources for adaptation.
Passive investors must balance cost efficiency and net-zero alignment, understanding that rigid benchmarks may sacrifice short-term return optimization for long-term climate goals.

Lesson 7: Discretionary and Quantitative Approaches to Managing Portfolios

Climate-focused funds dominate new sustainable fund launches in Europe, especially ETFs classified under SFDR Article 8 and 9.
Discretionary strategies are actively managed and include thematic, transition, stewardship, green bond, multi-asset, and carbon trading strategies.
Thematic strategies concentrate on sectors or solutions like clean energy, offering high-impact but higher volatility exposure.
Transition strategies target firms actively decarbonizing; stewardship strategies aim to influence high emitters via shareholder engagement.
Green bond strategies invest in use-of-proceeds-labeled bonds supporting climate projects; multi-asset strategies diversify across public and private markets.
Carbon trading strategies invest in regulated carbon markets (e.g., EU ETS), excluding voluntary carbon markets.
Quantitative strategies use rules-based models, constraints, and optimizations to manage portfolios based on climate data and carbon exposure.
Quant strategies benefit from objective, historical climate data (e.g., GHG emissions) but face limits due to shorter data history compared to financial data.
"Quantamental" methods blend quantitative filtering (e.g., CDP data, exclusion screens) with forward-looking ESG analysis to build thematic baskets.
Case studies include:
Nordea Global Climate (discretionary equity, Article 9) prioritizing green solution providers across three themes: energy, efficiency, and protection.
Ninety One Emerging Markets Transition Debt (discretionary credit) financing high-emitting EM companies committed to decarbonization.
Man Climate Impact Bond Fund (discretionary fixed income) uses IAMs to target underfunded transition sectors with measurable carbon impact.
Lombard Odier Electrification Fund (discretionary equity) identifies "green alpha" via electrification tipping points and TAMs.
Osmosis Resource Efficient Fund (quantitative equity) targets superior returns by optimizing for carbon, water, and waste efficiency.
Man Target Climate Fund (systematic multi-asset) combines cross-asset class alignment, SDG goals, and a proprietary risk overlay.


MODULE 2 - Managing Climate Risks, Emissions Attribution Methodologies

Lesson 1 - Techniques for Managing Climate Risks within Investment Portfolios

Carbon constraints help align portfolios with climate goals by excluding or underweighting high emitters; investors can use sector or issuer-specific exclusions or adopt Paris-Aligned Benchmark (PAB) rules.
Quantitative strategies can leverage the skewed distribution of emissions data to build balanced portfolios with limited tracking error while targeting lower-carbon exposure.
Hedging approaches include divesting from stranded assets, investing in climate mitigation or adaptation technologies, and reallocating toward companies better prepared for the transition.
Hedge funds and long-short strategies mitigate transition risk by going long on companies actively decarbonizing and short on laggards, enabling market-neutral climate exposure.
Strategies using climate news sentiment (e.g., via natural language processing) can identify long-short alpha signals, showing green firms tend to outperform brown ones during high climate attention periods.
Climate Value-at-Risk (VaR) models assess portfolio exposure to climate scenarios, estimating potential losses based on physical and transition risks under IPCC and NGFS scenarios.
Climate VaR enables capital allocation decisions, risk limit setting, and compliance with regulatory frameworks like EU SFDR and TCFD recommendations.
Scenario analysis tools such as climate-adjusted valuation (CAV) estimate P&L impacts under various climate pathways, supporting comparisons against benchmarks.
What-if modeling enables portfolio reallocation testing—e.g., shifting from higher-risk assets to more resilient ones—to improve physical and transition climate-adjusted outcomes.
Portfolio changes can be stress-tested across asset classes and rating segments, as illustrated in Exhibits 9–11 (pages 19–21), which show reduced climate loss impact with reallocation.

Lesson 2- Emissions Attribution Methodologies
Emissions attribution analysis identifies the drivers behind a portfolio’s carbon footprint and helps distinguish real decarbonization from portfolio rebalancing effects.
It provides a backward-looking tool to assess the effectiveness of climate transition plans and protect against greenwashing by promoting real-economy emissions reductions.
The approach is conceptually similar to traditional performance attribution (e.g., Brinson model), using categories like divested positions, reweighting, or company-level emissions changes.
Direct factors include real emissions changes from portfolio companies; indirect factors include ownership share shifts or portfolio restructuring.
Attribution is not yet effective for sovereign or real asset portfolios and is currently best applied to listed equity and corporate credit.
The method supports net-zero portfolio alignment by identifying which positions are contributing to carbon reductions and which are not.
The "theory of change" and double materiality frameworks highlight both financial materiality (impacts on the portfolio) and environmental/social materiality (portfolio’s impacts on the planet).
Hedge funds present unique attribution challenges due to short-selling and leverage; netting of long and short emissions may obscure true climate impact.
The IIGCC guidance recommends separating financial risk exposure from real-world climate impact and discourages emissions netting across long/short positions.
Portfolio leverage amplifies emissions exposure; strategies that rescale emissions data without full transparency may risk greenwashing.
Regulatory frameworks like the EU SFDR now provide guidance on short-selling and netting practices for emissions disclosure in hedge funds.


MODULE 3 - Engagement and Stewardship

Lesson 1  An Asset Owner’s Perspective on Climate Integration in Manager Selection

Long-term asset owners are increasingly integrating climate criteria into manager selection to fulfill both fiduciary duty and sustainability goals.
Asset owners now emphasize climate risk analysis as part of manager due diligence, including scrutiny of transition and physical risks, climate scenario modeling, and decarbonization strategies.
Climate considerations are included in Investment Policy Statements and tied to board accountability in jurisdictions like the UK; global uptake varies.
The Net Zero Investment Framework guides asset owners to decarbonize portfolios and allocate capital to climate solutions while assessing managers across asset classes.
The manager evaluation process includes four key steps:
Due diligence (e.g., climate scenario analysis, engagement policies),
Engagement (dialogue and legal agreements),
Annual review (tracking emissions, climate actions),
Benchmarking and reporting (comparing performance across peers and regions).
Climate-specific reviews assess carbon footprinting (Scopes 1–3), transition and physical risk, net-zero alignment, and impact metrics such as Scope 4 avoided emission.
Engagement expectations vary by asset class:
Public equity: Proxy voting and engagement;
Fixed income: Most effective via green bonds;
Private equity: Highly customizable via side letters;
Infrastructure/real estate: Naturally aligned with climate metrics;
Hedge funds: Minimal engagement possible.
Benchmarking helps identify climate leaders and laggards within multi-asset portfolios and promotes alignment on climate KPIs, especially in SFDR Article 9 and SDG-aligned funds.
CalPERS is highlighted as a leading case study—committed to net-zero, climate scenario integration, manager accountability, and a $100B climate solutions capital deployment plan


MODULE 4 - Module 4: Engagement and Stewardship

Lesson 1: Purpose and Benefits of Investor Engagement and Stewardship
Stewardship is “preserving and enhancing the value of assets with which one has been entrusted.”

Engagement is “purposeful dialogue with a specific and targeted objective to achieve change.”

Stewardship applies across all asset classes—not just equities—and involves purposeful dialogue aimed at preserving and enhancing long-term value.
Effective engagement requires clear objectives, direct access to decision-makers, persistence, and tracking progress against milestones.
Investors engage companies using a range of methods including private meetings, collaborative initiatives, formal letters, and coalitions.
Escalation strategies move from dialogue to more assertive actions like public campaigns, shareholder resolutions, and divestment.
Fixed income investors influence issuers by engaging during bond issuance and including ESG clauses in loan documentation.
Sovereign engagement is expanding, supported by tools that help assess and influence national climate policies.
Private equity, real estate, and infrastructure provide opportunities for direct ESG impact through board influence and asset-level strategies.
Success measurement is difficult; many investors use milestone-based models to track progress from issue identification to outcome.
There is a growing emphasis on engagement quality over quantity, as inflated interaction counts may not reflect actual impact.
Initiatives like Climate Action 100+ face criticism for limited short-term progress but are improving long-term governance and disclosure goals.
Political backlash, especially in the U.S., has led some managers to withdraw from climate coalitions, highlighting tensions in ESG engagement despite its strategic importance.


Lesson 2: Main Principles and Requirements of Stewardship Codes
Stewardship codes guide investor behavior through best practice or regulatory standards across over 20 global jurisdictions.
Most codes are modeled on the original 2010 UK Stewardship Code, featuring seven core principles: governance, conflict management, informed decision-making, monitoring, voting, collaboration, and transparency.
Codes often follow a "comply or explain" approach and differ in enforcement strength, especially between regulatory and investor-led codes.
Stewardship codes promote integration of ESG factors—including climate change—within investment and engagement policies, even if not always explicitly mentioned.
Conflicts of interest management is emphasized more in regulatory codes than investor-group codes, which sometimes downplay these obligations.
Monitoring includes tracking ESG risks alongside financial performance, treating issues like emissions and biodiversity loss as early indicators of value risk.
Engagement is expected when monitoring reveals concern, with codes promoting a range of escalation actions (e.g., dialogue, voting, divestment).
Voting should be informed and independent—codes discourage blind reliance on proxy advisers and stress responsibility in share recall and vote abstention practices.
Collective action is encouraged to improve influence and legitimacy but must respect legal boundaries on acting in concert.
Transparency is vital; investors must disclose their stewardship policies and report publicly on engagement activity and voting behavior.
Emerging themes in newer codes include:
Consideration of other stakeholders beyond shareholders,
Adequate skills and resources for stewardship delivery,
Delegation of stewardship while retaining accountability, and
Public policy engagement, particularly around systemic issues like climate change.
The UK Stewardship Code (2020) expanded from 7 to 12 principles for asset owners/managers and introduced 6 for service providers. It places strong emphasis on outcomes and integration of climate and ESG considerations.


Lesson 3: Strategies for Effective Shareholder Engagement and Active Ownership
Engagement progresses through various stages, often starting with direct dialogue between investors and companies. This private approach allows for trust-building and candid discussions away from public scrutiny.
Effective engagement involves meeting with key decision-makers, including board members, who have the authority to enact changes.
Recognizing cultural nuances is essential for successful engagement. Different regions and companies may require tailored approaches to communication and strategy alignment.
Engagement may escalate to more public actions such as voting at annual shareholder meetings and proposing resolutions.
Increasingly, investors focus on climate-related engagements, including “say-on-climate” resolutions and shareholder proposals.
Successful engagement requires persistence and consistency over time.


Lesson 4: Hypothetical Case Study: Following a Process for Engagement
Following a systematic process may result in effective engagement despite resource limitations.
Use of metrics like carbon footprint and industry benchmarks (e.g., Climate Action 100+) to prioritize engagement candidates aids in identification of targets.
It is important for investors to analyze reports and stakeholder feedback to identify risks and opportunities.
Collaborating within platforms like Climate Action 100+ to align objectives and enhance influence is essential to explore existing initiative.
To set clear objectives, it is essential that investors define measurable goals to drive company responsiveness and shareholder value.
Open discussions with decision-makers help to understand company dynamics and promote sustainability.
Investors should consider escalation carefully based on dialogue outcomes and stakeholder alignment.
Effective stewardship requires a disciplined approach balancing strategic objectives with ongoing company dialogue and action.
